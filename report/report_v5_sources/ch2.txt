=== CHAPTER 2 REVIEW OF LITERATURE
## 2.1 Literature Review
Trained battery models map voltage, current and temperature to internal states [8], LSTM networks predict the SOH [7], deep neural network models predict the remaining useful life [15], [16], and digital twins bring such models inside the BMS [17]. The opaque nature of these models is addressed by SHAP-attributed gradient boosting, making the system explainable [9], and by studies of the accuracy–transparency trade-off [10], while edge [11] and federated learning [12], [13], [18], [19] keep telemetry on board. Most of this work targets SOH on laboratory datasets. SOC estimators that are already trained are hardly ever compared with model-based ones under an uncertain initial SOC, and their output stays diagnostic rather than being informative about a trip decision.
Energy rather than distance is the binding constraint in electric-vehicle routing [3]. The trip energy depends on traffic [1], [2], topography [4], [5] and regeneration [6], and is handled by charging-aware routing [20] or by terrain-aware routing [21] for automated vehicles. Such methods plan the route or help in driving the vehicle, but they do not govern how a rider uses the drive modes. AMSA [14] switches modes in the vehicle control unit from the SOC, the instantaneous topographic gradient and the instantaneous power demand, and falls back to limp-home mode at low SOC. It was tested in model-in-the-loop simulation on a predefined urban drive cycle, and its range check reacts to a gradient only once the gradient occurs, without battery health, live traffic or the cumulative terrain ahead in the trip. As Table 2.1 shows, no surveyed work combines battery-state estimation with route terrain and live traffic into a pre-trip, leg-wise decision that enforces a drive mode or a charging stop.
H3: 2.1.1 Data-driven battery state estimation
Zhang et al. [8] review how sensing and artificial intelligence are converging into "smart batteries" and note that most learned battery models are trained and tested on laboratory cycling data rather than vehicle data. The LSTM network of Hochreiter and Schmidhuber [22], whose gated memory cell retains information over many time steps, is the recurrent architecture used in most learned battery estimators.
Xu, Xu and Zhu [7] estimate the SOH of lithium-ion cells with an LSTM extended by spatio-temporal attention. The model captures the temporal dependence of degradation but remains a black box and is not linked to vehicle operation. Liu et al. [15] predict remaining useful life from short discharging fragments with a hybrid deep-learning model; the prediction is diagnostic and not connected to trip planning. Li et al. [16] review neural networks for battery health and lifetime prediction and show that their accuracy depends on representative training data, which our own results confirm: an LSTM trained only on synthetic data performed poorly on the real pack until fine-tuned. Madani et al. [17] survey AI and digital-twin technologies for battery management systems, but do not address decisions about vehicle use.
H3: 2.1.2 Model-based SOC estimation and benchmark data
The Kalman filter [23] combines a model prediction with a measurement correction weighted by their uncertainties. Plett [24] applied the extended Kalman filter (EKF) to vehicle battery packs: Coulomb counting predicts the SOC and the terminal voltage corrects it through an open-circuit-voltage (OCV) curve and resistor–capacitor (RC) model. The EKF recovers from a wrong initial SOC but needs a characterized cell model. Xing et al. [28] estimated the SOC of LiFePO4 cells from the OCV at different temperatures, and He et al. [29] combined a neural network with an unscented Kalman filter. Both used the CALCE LiFePO4 dataset of the University of Maryland (DST, US06 and FUDS drive cycles at −10 to 50 °C), which is the public benchmark used in this project.
H3: 2.1.3 Explainable, edge and federated learning
SHAP [30] attributes a model's prediction to its inputs using Shapley values. Xiao et al. [9] explain an XGBoost SOH model with SHAP, and El Bahnasawi et al. [10] show that explainability is needed for operator trust; in both, the explanation is diagnostic and does not feed a control decision. A. R C et al. [11] run battery-health monitoring for electric scooters on an FPGA at the edge, without using the route or terrain. Federated learning keeps raw data on each vehicle and shares only model updates [12]; Abbaraju and Kundu [13], Wang et al. [19] and Bouchiha et al. [18] apply it to SOH estimation of connected EVs. None of these works acts on vehicle operation.
H3: 2.1.4 Uncertainty quantification
Gal and Ghahramani [25] showed that keeping dropout active at prediction time and repeating the prediction (Monte-Carlo dropout) approximates Bayesian uncertainty. Such estimates are often poorly calibrated; split-conformal prediction [26] uses a held-out calibration set to obtain intervals with guaranteed coverage. iBMS combines both to obtain a calibrated lower SOC bound.
H3: 2.1.5 Energy-aware routing and drive-mode adaptation
Zhu et al. [1] predict EV energy consumption from field data with machine learning, and Skuza et al. [2] model the effect of traffic parameters on consumption. Martin et al. [3] conclude that energy, not distance, is the binding constraint of EV routing. Lai et al. [4] include speed optimization and uneven topography in routing, Puma-Benavides et al. [5] report higher consumption on steep high-altitude urban roads, and Szumska [6] reviews regenerative braking and its limited recovery efficiency. Kirca and McGordon [27] report drag areas of about 0.50–0.59 m² for an upright electric-motorcycle rider, which supports the value used in our road-load model. Kapoor [20] routes EVs to charging stations with graph neural networks, and Ning et al. [21] plan energy-efficient trajectories on uneven terrain; these methods choose a route or control the vehicle fully but do not regulate a rider's drive modes.
The closest work is the AMSA of Pise, Yeolekar, Lokhande and Khan [14] from our department. It selects ECO, SPORT or limp-home mode in the vehicle control unit from the Coulomb-counted SOC, the instantaneous gradient and the power demand, prefers ECO below 40 % SOC and was evaluated in Simulink on a predefined 10 km Pune traffic profile. It reacts to a gradient only once it occurs and does not use battery health, live traffic or the terrain of the route ahead. Table 2.1 summarizes the literature.

## 2.2 Gap Identification
- Gap 1 – estimation and planning are separate: battery-state estimators [7], [9], [11], [13] do not know the trip ahead, and energy-aware routing [3], [4], [20] does not use the battery state, its health or its uncertainty.
- Gap 2 – no pre-trip, route-level drive-mode decision: no work combines battery state, route terrain and live traffic into a leg-wise decision that enforces a drive mode or a charging stop; AMSA [14] decides in real time from the instantaneous gradient.
- Gap 3 – weak evaluation and no uncertainty in the decision: learned SOC estimators are seldom compared with model-based and trivial baselines, and their uncertainty [25], [26] is not used in safety decisions.
iBMS addresses these gaps by checking the whole route before departure with a fairly benchmarked SOC estimate and deciding on its calibrated lower bound.
