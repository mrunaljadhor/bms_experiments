=== CHAPTER 5 RESULTS AND ANALYSIS
## 5.1 Functional Demonstration Outcomes
The complete system was demonstrated on the deployed website (https://ibmsdashboard.vercel.app), which calls the route-energy model and AMSA on the cloud backend; the backend queries TomTom for the route and traffic and OpenTopoData for the elevation. The demonstration uses the Dehradun → Mussoorie hill route, a 33 km climb of about 1,400 m.
FIG: figures/ui2_user_route.png | Fig. 5.1: User view during the demonstration: Dehradun → Mussoorie at 80 % SOC, with route-aware distance-to-empty, ECO-Lock and charging recommendation
In the User view (Fig. 5.1) the rider sets the SOC (80 %), enters origin and destination and calculates the route. The answer was CRITICAL: the route-aware range is 34.2 km in ECO but only 27.9 km in SPORT for a 33.3 km route, so the dashboard enforced ECO-Lock, disabled the SPORT button with an explanation and showed that ECO arrives with 0.9 km to spare. A flat-road estimate would have shown 61 km and allowed SPORT mode. The route-physics panel shows the values behind the decision: a cumulative climb of 1,385 m (grade 4.15 %), the traffic-jam ratio and the regenerative recovery.
FIG: figures/ui2_uncertainty.png | Fig. 5.2: SOC uncertainty card with the calibrated lower bound and the risk-aware AMSA decision
The Analyst view shows the state of health (94.3 %) and remaining useful life (11.7 years) with their contributing factors. The SOC uncertainty card (Fig. 5.2) shows the estimate and its calibrated lower bound. With risk-aware AMSA switched on at 75 % SOC, the decision SOC became 67.2 % (margin 7.8 points), below the 69.9 % needed, and the decision changed from ECO-Lock to a required charging stop.
## 5.2 Test Results and Performance Evaluation
H3: 5.2.1 SOC estimation

On held-out synthetic scenarios the LSTM reached an MAE of 0.20 % (R² = 0.9997), which is the best case, because in synthetic data voltage almost directly gives SOC; the generator ties voltage to SOC almost deterministically. Zero-shot on the field data the MAE rose to 7.37 %, because the real battery's voltage curve differs from the synthetic one. LOSO fine-tuning on five sessions cut the error by 83 % to 1.24 % (0.49–1.40 % per session, and 3.39 % for the parallel-SPORT session, whose SOC lies at the edge of the training range).
Coulomb counting with the true SOC₀ gave 0.07 %, because each session lasts under 5 min; a +2 % initial error stayed in the estimate throughout (2.06 %). The EKF reached 2.42 % for either SOC₀. On the flat LiFePO4 voltage plateau, and with an OCV curve not measured on this pack, its voltage correction is weak and it settles on a biased estimate. A constant predictor of the mean training SOC reaches 1.22 %, on par with the fine-tuned LSTM, although it always outputs the same SOC. Each session moves only 0.2–0.4 % SOC, so the data cannot tell which method tracks better. The field data therefore show that fine-tuning removes the synthetic-to-real offset, but they cannot rank the estimators; SOC tracking is shown only on full discharges.
CALCE data matter because a full discharge is there, where the constant predictor fails (24.65 %). On CALCE, the LSTM tracked SOC on unseen cycles with 3.17 % at 25 °C and 2.4–4.1 % across 0–50 °C, using one model and no SOC₀, OCV curve or capacity (Fig. 5.3). Its largest errors are short spikes at abrupt load steps. The EKF with a measured OCV and an identified RC model was more accurate (0.96 %) and recovered from a 10 % initial error, but it needs pack-specific characterization that was not available for the test vehicle. This is the trade-off between the two approaches.
H3: 5.2.2 Route feasibility (AMSA)
AMSA was evaluated on four route pairs in both directions (New Delhi ↔ Noida, Dehradun ↔ Mussoorie, Shimla ↔ Kufri, Siliguri ↔ Darjeeling), queried from TomTom on 16 October 2026 with 60 km/h and SOH = 100 %, sweeping the initial SOC from 5 to 100 %. Different lengths (13–60 km) as well as hill climbs of about 700 m to 2.3 km gave mild and extreme cases. It was compared with a naive estimator using the same pack and consumption on a flat road, which models a conventional range display (Table 5.2).

Table 5.2 lists the SOC thresholds at which the AMSA decision changes, and Fig. 5.4 plots the DTE curves. On the three uphill routes, the cumulative climb cut the full-charge ECO range from the naive 76.1 km to 47.6–49.6 km, so the naive estimate overstates the range by 53–60 %. The required ECO energy was 1,371 Wh against a naive 858 Wh for Dehradun → Mussoorie: the display says 43.7 % SOC is enough, but actually a rider needs 69.9 % (+60 %). Similarly, Shimla → Kufri needs 536 Wh against 345 Wh (+55 %), and Siliguri → Darjeeling 2,388 Wh against 1,558 Wh (+53 %), which exceeds the 1,962 Wh pack, so it is infeasible even from a full charge. The minimum SOC to arrive in ECO mode rose by 9.7, 26.2 and 42.3 percentage points on these routes.
Over the SOC sweep, the naive estimator judged Dehradun → Mussoorie reachable at 26 SOC levels (43.7–69.9 %) at which AMSA returned IMPOSSIBLE. The counts were 21 for Siliguri → Darjeeling (79.4–100 %), 10 for Shimla → Kufri and one each for the two flat directions and Kufri → Shimla. A rider trusting the naive display would start each of these trips although the model predicts that it cannot be completed; AMSA instead requires a charging stop or, inside the ECO-Lock band, enforces ECO mode so that the trip stays feasible. On descents the naive estimate is conservative: Mussoorie → Dehradun needed 585 Wh against a naive 731 Wh (−20 %), as regeneration recovers part of the descent energy, which is opposite to the uphill scenario. The display is too cautious because regeneration gives energy back. On the flat pair the two estimates differed by at most 2 percentage points, so both the display and the model agree. Repeating the study on 18 and 21 October changed the hill-route ranges by at most 0.8 km and the thresholds by at most 1 point.
H3: 5.2.3 Edge deployment, explainability and uncertainty
Edge deployment: dynamic-range int8 quantization in TensorFlow Lite reduced the model from 385.7 KiB (Keras) to 67.6 KiB, which is 5.7× smaller, with the same accuracy, with a synthetic MAE of 0.199 % against 0.203 % before quantization. Median single-thread inference took 0.41 ms on the laptop CPU, against 3.05 ms for compiled and 124.5 ms for eager Keras. Full-integer conversion of this LSTM failed in the converter used, and microcontroller deployment is still to be tested.
Explainability: SHAP [30] attributions were computed on the LSTM with expected gradients and 200 background windows. Voltage carried 96.1 % of the attribution magnitude on synthetic data and 92.8 % on field data, current about 3 %, and temperature 0.9 % and 4.2 %. The last 10 of the 50 steps, that is mostly the last 10 seconds, held 64.6 % (synthetic) and 71.1 % (field) of the attribution, which also makes sense. The network thus reads SOC mainly from recent terminal voltage, using the voltage–SOC relationship it learned, which is physically plausible and explains the zero-shot gap in Table 5.1, because the voltage–SOC relation differs between the synthetic and the real pack.
Uncertainty and risk-aware AMSA: on CALCE (unseen cycles, 25 °C), the Monte-Carlo mean reached 3.24 %, close to the deterministic 3.17 %, showing that estimating uncertainty costs no accuracy. A nominal 95 % Gaussian dropout interval covered only 83.5 % of the reference SOC, showing that the model is overconfident and its raw intervals cannot be trusted. Conformal calibration made the intervals reliable: the two-sided interval covered 96.6 %, and the lower bound was violated in 2.1 % of windows (4.3 % over 0–50 °C and 7.6–10 % at 0–10 °C). The mean lower margin μ − SOC_lb was 7.8 points. To measure the effect on decisions, the ECO thresholds of the eight routes in Table 5.2 were applied to every test window (Table 5.3).
TABLE: Table 5.3: Effect of the SOC used by AMSA on decision errors (eight routes, CALCE 25 °C test windows)
AMSA decides on | Unsafe acceptances (%) | False refusals (%)
Point estimate μ | 0.71 | 1.59
Calibrated lower bound SOC_lb | 0.007 | 8.15

Deciding on the point estimate accepted 0.71 % of the windows in which the trip was infeasible and refused 1.59 % of the feasible ones. Deciding on SOC_lb cut unsafe acceptances to 0.007 % and raised false refusals to 8.15 %: fewer accepted trips in exchange for two orders of magnitude fewer stranding decisions caused by SOC error. This trade-off is needed for safety, as being told to charge when it was not needed can be annoying sometimes, but it is a lot better than being stranded on hilly routes.

H3: 5.3.3 Sensitivity and explainability
FIG: ../experiments/exp4_dte_sensitivity/fig_dte_tornado.png | Fig. 5.5: One-at-a-time sensitivity of the DTE (ECO, 80 % SOC, flat road, 60 km/h; baseline 60.8 km)
A one-at-a-time sensitivity analysis of (4.7) was run around ECO mode, 80 % SOC, a flat road without traffic and 60 km/h, for which DTE = 60.8 km (Fig. 5.5). Speed matters the most because drag grows with speed squared: 40 km/h raised DTE by 76.6 % and 90 km/h lowered it by 49.9 %, since aerodynamic drag is about 80 % of the road load at 60 km/h. A 5 % uphill grade cut DTE by 42.9 %, and a 5 % downhill grade added 31.6 %. Ageing to 80 % SOH cut it by 20 %, and an acceleration aggressiveness of 50 % or a 15 °C ambient deviation each cut it by 7.0 %. A ±10 % change in $c_0$ moved DTE by +11.1 %/−9.1 %. The relative results, including the 53–60 % overestimation on climbs, do not depend on this calibration, while absolute DTE scales with 1/$c_0$. Siliguri → Darjeeling stays infeasible from a full charge for any flat-road ECO range below 92 km, above the 84.6 km upper end of the ±10 % band. Raising J from 0 to 0.8 increased DTE by 60.5 %; Section 5.4 discusses this result. Fig. 5.6 shows that the LSTM relies mainly on the terminal voltage.

## 5.4 Interpretation of Results
Terrain decides trip feasibility as much as pack capacity does for a light two-wheeler. On flat urban routes an SOC-proportional estimate is adequate, but on mountain climbs it overestimates the ECO range by more than half and accepts trips that the model predicts will strand the rider, across SOC bands up to 26 percentage points wide. Live mode switching [14] cannot avoid this, because it reacts only once the climb has started. iBMS calculates the whole route before starting the trip, and either ECO-Lock is enforced from the start or a charging stop is required before the beginning of the trip. On descents the naive estimate is conservative, so SPORT mode can be released at a lower SOC. The cost is one routing request, one elevation request, a closed-form evaluation of (4.4)–(4.8) and a 0.41 ms SOC inference.
Traffic is one of the parameters that need attention. In the model, congestion lowers the speed, and since drag dominates at 60 km/h, the energy consumption per km falls. Electric vehicles in general are known to be more efficient at low speed. The losses of repeated starts are possibly underestimated by the stop-and-go factor in (4.7), so under heavy congestion the range gain is an upper bound.
On the SOC side, the field data show only offset removal, while CALCE shows real tracking; the EKF is more accurate when the cell is characterized, while the LSTM needs no initial SOC, OCV curve or capacity, so the choice is a trade-off. Conformal calibration restored the expected coverage, and deciding on the lower bound cut unsafe acceptances by two orders of magnitude at the cost of more refusals, the preferred trade-off for a safety function.
